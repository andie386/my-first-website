Beyond the Stereotype: Discrimination Against Indigenous Students and Workers

Igorot students and workers still face prejudice in classrooms, learning materials, and workplaces. Explore what it looks like, where it comes from, and what can change it.

The issue: Indigenous identity is treated as a reason for lesser respect

“Igorot” is the collective name for Indigenous groups of the Cordillera, such as the Ibaloy, Bontok, Tingguian, and Ifugao. Click a card for details.

2021textbook controversy
A learning material depicting Igorots offensively drew condemnation from DepEd-CAR’s director and lawmakers. A Baguio councilor called the pattern institutional discrimination (SunStar, Inquirer).
36Igorots in a Benguet study
Adonis and Couch gathered stories from 36 Igorots of different ages and work backgrounds about schooling and discrimination.
11 millionIndigenous Peoples nationwide
NCIP documented about 11 million IPs as of 2013 (cited by TESDA). This is a national figure, not only the Cordillera.

How it shows up

  • Igorot students are often received negatively by classmates when they say they are Indigenous, and some are called derogatory names (Adonis and Couch).
  • Learning materials have misrepresented Igorots, so the stereotype can be taught in class (SunStar, Inquirer).
  • Schools can push values of individual competition that clash with communal Indigenous values, forcing students to choose between fitting in and being marginalized (Adonis and Couch).
  • UP researchers note that Indigenous children too often face discrimination in schools.
  • International Alert says the systemic disempowerment of Muslims and Indigenous Peoples shows up as discriminatory hiring and employment practices.
  • Exclusionary workplace culture means minority employees may not feel able to express their identity.
  • A study abstract on Indigenous Peoples and employment (ILO-linked) states that Indigenous Peoples have a long history of discrimination, which can feed poverty.
  • Everyday remarks such as “Igorot ka? Hindi halata” or “Taga bundok ka?” are reported by Igorots in Baguio (Inquirer).

Data gap:We found no regional survey measuring how common discrimination is for Cordillera Indigenous students or workers. The evidence here is qualitative (studies, official statements, news reports). A small, ethical survey could fill that gap.

Myth vs reality

Igorots are backward or inferior.

This image traces back to colonial-era portrayals of Igorots as “primitive” (Scott, cited by Adonis and Couch). A Baguio councilor said it keeps resurfacing despite constitutional safeguards. NCIP and DepEd officials themselves call it a misportrayal to be corrected.

Igorots belong only in the mountains.

UP Baguio’s Prof. Tindaan wrote that Igorots are still perceived as a static group bound to the mountains, even in a mobile world. Her research looked at Igorots who migrated to the United Kingdom.

“Indigenous” means “indigent.”

NCIP-CAR’s director said some people treat the two words as synonyms. IPRA defines Indigenous Peoples by descent, distinct culture, and resistance to colonization, not by income.

You can tell who is Igorot by how they look.

IPRA’s definition includes self-ascription, meaning people identify themselves as Indigenous. A Baguio editor called the remark “It doesn’t show” offensive (Inquirer).

Why does this problem exist?

1. Colonial-era “primitive” imagery that never fully went away

Adonis and Couch trace the American-era “civilizing mission” and imagery that links Igorots with the “primitive” and lowlanders with the “modern.” That divide still shapes how classmates and others treat Igorots.

2. Misrepresentation in textbooks, films, and art

NCIP-CAR’s director has said Igorots face discrimination because of wrong portrayal in textbooks, artwork, and films. DepEd explained that it vets materials for public schools but does not control private publishers. The 2021 worksheet was written by a teacher far from the Cordillera and sat in a DepEd portal awaiting quality assessment.

3. Low awareness of IPRA and Indigenous rights

A forum on youth human rights issues in the Cordillera listed lack of awareness of IPRA and IP rights, and misrepresentation of traditional practices, among the concerns.

4. Systemic exclusion in schools and workplaces

A councilor and a youth leader described the problem as institutional and systemic, saying DepEd alone cannot fix it. International Alert links the systemic disempowerment of Indigenous Peoples to discriminatory hiring and exclusionary workplace culture.

5. Weak enforcement and gaps in general anti-discrimination law

IPRA has a section on unlawful acts pertaining to employment, yet discrimination persists. Separate equality and non-discrimination bills based on race and ethnicity have been filed in Congress (check their current status).

What can viably address it?

The causes sit in schools, media, and workplaces, so the solution is a package. Most pieces below are already proposed or tested by Cordillera officials, teachers, and researchers.

Education

Accurate, locally made lessons

Baguio’s council urged DepEd to fund research under its IP education program. Cordillera teachers offered to write lessons on the genuine Igorot way of life.

Accountability

Better vetting and public reporting

DepEd cannot control private publishers, so checks need to cover them too (proposal). Citizens were invited to post errors using the hashtag #depederrorwatch.

Schools

Culturally responsive schooling

At an Indigenous school in Mindoro, students said the culturally responsive approach restored their self-esteem and motivation (UP). This is an example from outside the Cordillera.

Workplaces

Inclusive hiring and culture

International Alert’s “Red Flags” guide helps employers spot exclusion and build accessible workplaces for religious and ethnic communities.

Law

Enforce and strengthen the law

IPRA lists unlawful acts in employment, and equality bills based on race and ethnicity have been filed in Congress. Awareness of these rights helps people report violations.

Voice

Indigenous-led storytelling

Netizens answered the 2021 insult by sharing images of their own attire. Telling the story in Indigenous voices counters the stereotype directly.

What you can do: learn the proper names of Cordillera groups, challenge stereotyping remarks, report errors in learning materials, and amplify Indigenous creators.
